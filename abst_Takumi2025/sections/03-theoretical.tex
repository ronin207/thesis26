\section{The Field-Mismatch Tax and When a Precompile Pays}
We formalize the \emph{field-mismatch tax}: the cost that arises when a primitive's natural algebraic domain (here a large prime field $\mathbb{F}_q$) does not coincide with the proof system's \emph{native} arithmetic. A backend is modelled by its native field $\mathbb{F}_p$, an encoding of the computation into $\mathbb{F}_p$, and a constraint representation (R1CS for static circuits, AIR/trace for STARK-based zkVMs). In the \emph{field-aligned} regime of application-specific circuits the designer matches $\mathbb{F}_p$ to the primitive's domain, so arithmetic is expressed directly and constraint growth
tracks multiplicative complexity. In the \emph{mismatch} regime of general-purpose zkVMs the native field is fixed and much smaller, so each $\mathbb{F}_q$ element is carried as $k=\lceil \log_2 q / w\rceil$ $w$-bit limbs with carry propagation and modular reduction.

A counting argument gives a lower bound: a single non-native multiplication forces $\Omega(k)$ native multiplications, since the product depends on all $k$ input limbs while each native multiply carries at most one. For STARK provers this amplifies to a $\Theta(k^2)$ inflation of the schoolbook-emulated trace, and since prover time scales roughly as $O(W\,T\log T)$ in trace width $W$ and length $T$, the tax is a structural slowdown, not a constant-factor one. This yields the criterion: a precompile lowers cost only on the field-mismatch axis, and pays only when it removes more emulated trace area than the dedicated chip adds. A primitive already served by an existing precompile, or native to the prover field, therefore does not warrant a bespoke chip even though one could be built.
