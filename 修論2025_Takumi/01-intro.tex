\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
\subsection{Background}
Post-quantum anonymous credentials must satisfy three requirements at once: they must be verifiable, privacy-preserving, and long-lived. The credibility of digital credentials is under strain: forged admission letters have driven student deportations~\cite{bbc2023canada}, and the $2021$ LinkedIn scrape put approximately $700$ million user profiles up for sale~\cite{nixon2021linkedin}, so a credential system must be at once \emph{verifiable} and \emph{privacy-preserving}. This is now a regulatory requirement: the European Digital Identity framework (eIDAS~2.0, Regulation~(EU)~2024/1183) mandates privacy-preserving attribute attestation with unlinkability for the EU Digital Identity Wallet~\cite{eidas2024}. A credential meeting it must also be \emph{long-lived}, secure against adversaries with large-scale quantum computers, ruling out classical anonymous-credential constructions whose security reduces to discrete-logarithm or RSA~\cite{fips204,fips205}. Constructions meeting all three requirements at once are only now emerging~\cite{jeudy2023lattice,10.1007/978-981-96-0957-4_3}.

\subsection{From Constructing to Deploying}\label{ssec:deploy-question}
Any such credential proves, in zero knowledge, that one \emph{knows} a valid post-quantum signature without revealing the signature itself, the message, or the key. The most arithmetically efficient candidates are Loquat~\cite{cryptoeprint:2024/868} and its successor PLUM~\cite{10.1007/978-981-95-2961-2_6}, two post-quantum signature schemes built on power-residue pseudorandom functions (PRFs), in a single family with a shared verification template (Section~\ref{sec:prelim}). \emph{Designing} such schemes is the question those works take up; so we take up how to \emph{deploy} a member of this family on the machine the credential holder owns, and at what cost.

The dominant approach compiles the verification predicate into a fixed arithmetic circuit and proves it with a transparent zkSNARK such as Aurora~\cite{cryptoeprint:2018/828}. This specialisation is efficient, but a deployment is not static: the signature is updated as standards mature, parameters are retuned, and disclosure policies evolve. This last kind of change is intrinsic to how anonymous credentials are used. In a system such as BDEC~\cite{10.1007/978-981-96-0957-4_3} the holder of a credential proves a predicate about the attributes inside it, a threshold on a grade, an equality on a degree, or a conjunction drawn over several credentials, and the predicates a deployment must support are settled by its verifiers over time rather than fixed once at design. A circuit fixed at deployment time must be regenerated and re-audited whenever one of these predicates changes. A zero-knowledge virtual machine (zkVM) is one way to keep the predicate flexible: a single universal relation proves the correct execution of an ordinary program~\cite{cryptoeprint:2023/1032,yang2026sokzkvm}, so a new predicate becomes a program edit rather than a new circuit. This thesis runs BDEC's credential relations on that substrate and identifies the challenges that surface, at a proving cost on consumer hardware that the literature has not measured.

In exchange for that generality, a zkVM pays a cost on algebraic primitives whose native arithmetic does not match the prover's field. Every member of the family above computes over a large ($199$-bit) prime field, while modern zkVM provers operate over $31$-bit native fields. Each large-field operation must therefore be emulated by multi-limb arithmetic over the small field, a cost we formalise in Section~\ref{sec:theoretical} as the \emph{field-mismatch tax}. Without mitigation the tax is decisive: precompile-free standalone PLUM verification does not complete on the target machine, running over five hours without terminating (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}), where the binding resource is time rather than memory.

\subsection{The Precompile Suite, and the Question It Answers}\label{ssec:question}
SP1~\cite{succinct2024sp1} permits \emph{application-defined precompiles}, dedicated AIR (algebraic intermediate representation) sub-circuits for designated primitives that the guest invokes as syscalls. A precompile absorbs the field-mismatch tax for a primitive that would otherwise dominate the trace. This thesis builds a suite of them for PLUM verification; that suite is the instrument this thesis studies, not its headline result. Building it also turns a non-terminating emulation into a finite proof whose cost can be attributed and bounded. The bespoke algebraic-hash precompile proves \emph{slower} than a standard-hash control, so whether a given precompile pays is itself a question the construction forces, nested inside the central question of this thesis:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Can a post-quantum anonymous credential run inside a zkVM on consumer hardware without breaking the security properties it must provide, and if it cannot run natively, what makes it run?}
\end{quote}

We answer it in layers, and the feasibility layer is priced by a trace-area cost model (Section~\ref{sec:theoretical}) that weighs the chip cells a precompile commits against the core cycles it removes, as a function of the field-mismatch width. This cost prices the feasibility layer. The security layer, which properties survive the move, is settled by the preservation theorem and the dual obstruction of Section~\ref{sec:security}. The credential construction (BDEC~\cite{10.1007/978-981-96-0957-4_3}) instantiated with PLUM is the concrete workload through which the criterion is calibrated and measured on SP1. The decision the criterion serves belongs to the operator of a real deployment, such as a university consortium choosing a proving substrate for digital diplomas today, who needs to know both what the precompile delivers and where it stops.

\subsection{Contributions of This Thesis}
This thesis shows that verifying a power-residue-PRF post-quantum signature does not run efficiently inside a zkVM, builds the precompile suite that makes it run, distils a reusable cost criterion for when such a precompile pays, and analyses which of the credential's security properties survive the move, locating the one that does not, deployable post-quantum anonymity. The contributions are as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A precompile suite for PLUM verification over a $199$-bit prime.} We build three AIR precompiles over the same field, a Griffin permutation (PLUM's algebraic hash), a field-multiplication chip, and a composed power-residue-PRF chip, each with a soundness argument tying its constraints to the reference specification (the constraint-level chip-equivalence is partially discharged; Section~\ref{sec:security}). The Griffin chip is what recovers feasibility.

  \item \textbf{A cost criterion for when a precompile pays, and the hash-cost inversion it predicts.} The bespoke algebraic-hash Griffin-$\mathbb{F}_p^{192}$ precompile~\cite{cryptoeprint:2022/403} proves \emph{slower} than a field-matched software SHA-3 control at the measured $\lambda{=}80$ (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}). The trace-area cost model of Section~\ref{sec:theoretical} postdicts the \emph{sign} of this inversion, a precompile pays exactly when the core trace area it removes exceeds the chip area it adds, and yields a falsifiable matched-field forecast as its keystone test. The result is reusable: anyone building a precompile for another primitive in another zkVM can decide from the specification whether it will pay. Across the suite the criterion separates three cases: Griffin pays against emulation yet loses against the field-matched control; the field-multiplication chip does not pay because the host already serves it; and the power-residue symbol, irreducibly large-prime, pays on total committed trace area (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}).

  \item \textbf{The dual obstruction to post-quantum anonymity, and the everlasting-anonymity boundary.} Knowledge soundness transfers to the zkVM substrate; deployable post-quantum zero-knowledge does not, and the obstruction is genuinely dual. The feasible, post-quantum-sound proof, the bare succinct STARK receipt, is not zero-knowledge (Horn~1); and the zero-knowledge wrap the toolchains ship, though it completes within the consumer memory budget, is pairing-based and hence not post-quantum (Horn~2). Feasibility is therefore not the obstruction; a limit of the available primitives is. Pushing the question to its information-theoretic limit, we prove that \emph{everlasting} anonymity cannot be established in the standard model for the class of hash-committed showings this construction uses, and give the conditional route out (Section~\ref{sec:security}). The obstruction is formalised in Section~\ref{sec:security} and measured in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}.

  \item \textbf{The first empirical characterisation of PLUM-in-a-zkVM.} We express the credential-generation (CreGen) and showing (ShowCre) relations as SP1 guests with PLUM in place of Loquat, and run standalone PLUM verification, in both execute and prove mode (all figures with full configuration in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}). To our knowledge this is the first such characterisation of PLUM verification inside a zkVM. \end{enumerate}

\noindent\emph{Security scope.} We give one conditional security-preservation theorem for PLUM-in-zkVM (Section~\ref{sec:security}), bounding the EUF-CMA, knowledge-soundness, and anonymity advantages of the zkVM-executed credential system, and resting on five explicitly named open assumptions (Section~\ref{sec:security}).

\subsection{Thesis Organisation}
The thesis proceeds as follows: related work (Section~\ref{sec:related}) and primitives (Section~\ref{sec:prelim}); the cost model and update churn (Section~\ref{sec:theoretical}); the construction and precompile suite (Section~\ref{sec:system}); the security-preservation theorem and its open assumptions (Section~\ref{sec:security}); the measurements (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}); the deployment decision framework (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}); and the conclusion (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}).
