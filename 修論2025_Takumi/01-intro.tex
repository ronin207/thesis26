\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
\subsection{Background}
Post-quantum anonymous credentials must satisfy three requirements at once: they must be verifiable, privacy-preserving, and long-lived. The credibility of digital credentials is under strain: forged admission letters have driven student deportations~\cite{bbc2023canada}, and the $2021$ LinkedIn scrape put approximately $700$ million user profiles up for sale~\cite{nixon2021linkedin}, so a credential system must be at once \emph{verifiable} and \emph{privacy-preserving}. This is now a regulatory requirement: the European Digital Identity framework (eIDAS~2.0, Regulation~(EU)~2024/1183) mandates privacy-preserving attribute attestation with unlinkability for the EU Digital Identity Wallet~\cite{eidas2024}. A credential meeting it must also be \emph{long-lived}, secure against adversaries with large-scale quantum computers, ruling out classical anonymous-credential constructions whose security reduces to discrete-logarithm or RSA~\cite{fips204,fips205}. Constructions meeting all three requirements at once are only now emerging~\cite{jeudy2023lattice,10.1007/978-981-96-0957-4_3}.

\subsection{From Constructing to Deploying}\label{ssec:deploy-question}
Any such credential proves, in zero knowledge, that one \emph{knows} a valid post-quantum signature without revealing the signature itself, the message, or the key. The most arithmetically efficient candidates are the Legendre-PRF scheme Loquat~\cite{cryptoeprint:2024/868} and its successor PLUM~\cite{10.1007/978-981-95-2961-2_6}, which replaces the Legendre PRF with a $t$-th power-residue PRF and FRI with STIR. PLUM generalises Loquat (at $t=2$ the power-residue PRF is exactly the Legendre PRF), so the two form a single family with a shared verification template. \emph{Designing} such schemes is the question those works take up; so we work towards answering the question of how to \emph{deploy} a member of this family on the machine the credential holder owns, and at what cost.

The dominant approach compiles the verification predicate into a fixed arithmetic circuit and proves it with a transparent zkSNARK such as Aurora~\cite{cryptoeprint:2018/828}. This specialisation is efficient, but a deployment is not static: the signature is updated as standards mature, parameters are retuned, and disclosure policies evolve. This last kind of change is intrinsic to how anonymous credentials are used. In a system such as BDEC~\cite{10.1007/978-981-96-0957-4_3} the holder of a credential proves a predicate about the attributes inside it, a threshold on a grade, an equality on a degree, or a conjunction drawn over several credentials, and the predicates a deployment must support are settled by its verifiers over time rather than fixed once at design. A circuit fixed at deployment time must be regenerated and re-audited whenever one of these predicates changes. A zero-knowledge virtual machine (zkVM) is one way to keep the predicate flexible: a single universal relation proves the correct execution of an ordinary program~\cite{cryptoeprint:2023/1032,yang2026sokzkvm}, so a new predicate becomes a program edit rather than a new circuit. This thesis works to make BDEC's credential relations run on that substrate and to identify the challenges that surface; which substrate to choose for deployment on consumer hardware, and whether the resulting proving cost is practical, is a live question the literature has not measured.

In exchange for that generality, a zkVM pays a cost on algebraic primitives whose native arithmetic does not match the prover's field. Every member of the family above computes over a large prime field (PLUM's nominal $192$-bit, concretely $199$-bit prime of Remark~\ref{rem:prime-substitution}; the quadratic extension $\mathbb{F}_{p^2}$ of the $127$-bit Mersenne prime in Loquat), while modern zkVM provers operate over $31$-bit native fields (KoalaBear, $2^{31}-2^{24}+1$, in the SP1 fork evaluated here). Each large-field operation must therefore be emulated by multi-limb arithmetic over the small field, a cost we formalise in Section~\ref{sec:theoretical} as the \emph{field-mismatch tax}. Without mitigation the tax is decisive: precompile-free standalone PLUM verification does not complete on the target machine, running over five hours without terminating (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}), where the binding resource is time rather than memory.

\subsection{The Precompile Suite, and the Question It Answers}\label{ssec:question}
SP1~\cite{succinct2024sp1} permits \emph{application-defined precompiles}, dedicated AIR sub-circuits for designated primitives that the guest invokes as syscalls. A precompile absorbs the field-mismatch tax for a primitive that would otherwise dominate the trace. This thesis builds a suite of them for PLUM verification; that suite is the instrument this thesis studies, not its headline result. Building it also turns a non-terminating emulation into a finite proof whose cost can be attributed and bounded, which lets us ask the question the suite exists to answer. One of our findings is that the bespoke algebraic-hash precompile proves \emph{slower} than a standard-hash control, so whether a given precompile pays is itself a question the construction forces, and it sits inside the larger one this thesis sets out to answer.

\begin{quote}
\emph{Can a post-quantum anonymous credential run inside a zkVM on consumer hardware without breaking the security properties it must provide, and if it cannot run natively, what makes it run?}
\end{quote}

We answer it in layers, and the feasibility layer is priced by a trace-area cost model (Section~\ref{sec:theoretical}) that weighs the chip cells a precompile commits against the core cycles it removes, as a function of the field-mismatch width. This cost prices the feasibility layer. The security layer, which properties survive the move, is settled by the preservation theorem and the deployability wall of Section~\ref{sec:security}. The credential construction (BDEC~\cite{10.1007/978-981-96-0957-4_3}) instantiated with PLUM is the concrete workload through which the criterion is calibrated and measured on SP1. The decision the criterion serves belongs to the operator of a real deployment, such as a university consortium choosing a proving substrate for digital diplomas today, who needs to know both what the precompile delivers and where it stops.

\subsection{Contributions of This Thesis}
This thesis shows that verifying a power-residue-PRF post-quantum signature does not run efficiently inside a zkVM, builds the precompile suite that makes it run, and analyses which of the credential's security properties survive the move, locating the one that does not, deployable post-quantum anonymity. The contributions are as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A precompile suite for PLUM verification over a $199$-bit prime.} We build three AIR precompiles over the same field, a Griffin permutation, a field-multiplication chip, and a composed power-residue-PRF chip, each with a soundness argument tying its constraints to the reference specification and each core-smoke-proved inside SP1. The Griffin chip among the three is what turns precompile-free PLUM verification, which does not terminate within the time budget on the target machine, into a finite proof whose cost the remaining contributions attribute and bound; the suite as a whole is the instrument on which the cost criterion is calibrated.

  \item \textbf{A cost criterion for when a precompile pays, and the hash-cost inversion it predicts.} The bespoke algebraic-hash Griffin-$\mathbb{F}_p^{192}$ precompile~\cite{cryptoeprint:2022/403} proves \emph{slower} than a software SHA-3 control at matched security, $14.24$ versus $13.24$~min, a factor of ${\approx}1.08$ in wall time and $1.13$ in cycles at $n{=}5$, $\lambda{=}80$. The trace-area cost model of Section~\ref{sec:theoretical} postdicts the \emph{sign} of this inversion, a precompile pays exactly when the core trace area it removes exceeds the chip area it adds, and yields a falsifiable matched-field forecast as its keystone test. This is the reusable result. Anyone building a precompile for another primitive in another zkVM can decide from the specification whether it will pay. Across the suite the criterion already separates three cases. Griffin pays, the field-multiplication chip does not because the host already serves it, and the power-residue symbol, which is irreducibly large-prime, is its open positive case with a specified deciding experiment.

  \item \textbf{The deployability wall, a dual obstruction to post-quantum anonymity.} Knowledge soundness transfers to the zkVM substrate; deployable post-quantum zero-knowledge does not. The zero-knowledge wrap now runs to completion within the consumer memory budget, so feasibility is not the obstruction. What remains is a fact about the primitive: the only zero-knowledge wraps the toolchains ship are pairing-based, hence not post-quantum. The obstruction is formalised in Section~\ref{sec:security} and measured in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}.

  \item \textbf{The first empirical characterisation of PLUM-in-a-zkVM.} We express the credential-generation (CreGen) relation as an SP1 guest with PLUM in place of Loquat, with the guest committing its public statement $x_{\mathsf{cre}}$ to the journal (statement-bound; Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}), benchmark the ShowCre relation, the $k{+}2$-verification generalisation, as a cost workload (now statement-bound as well, but a succinct core prove, so it prices verification rather than realising an anonymity instance), and run standalone PLUM verification on SP1. In execute mode the relations validate and confirm the additive cost model; in prove mode standalone PLUM verification and both credential relations complete a succinct, non-zero-knowledge prove on SP1 (all figures with full configuration in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}). To our knowledge this is the first execute- and prove-mode characterisation of PLUM verification inside a zkVM. \end{enumerate}

\noindent\emph{Security scope.} We give one conditional security-preservation theorem for PLUM-in-zkVM (Section~\ref{sec:security}), bounding the EUF-CMA, knowledge-soundness, and anonymity advantages against the zkVM-executed credential system in terms of five leaf errors (a sixth, key-privacy, entering only the issuance-anonymity bound), and resting on four explicitly named open assumptions: Griffin-AIR constraint soundness, PLUM signature-transcript simulatability, the quantum-random-oracle lift of the Fiat--Shamir step, and PLUM signature key-privacy (required only by issuance anonymity).
